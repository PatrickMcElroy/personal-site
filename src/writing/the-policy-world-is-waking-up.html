The professed business model of Anthropic is a simple yet ridiculous one on its face. Make the serving of each model profitable while sinking as much money as possible into scaling the training of your next one. If you are unprofitable on the whole, you can take comfort in the profitable serving of your most capable model, which hopefully exists at the very frontier of capability. Having a public model that is considered the very best is priceless, for a multitude of reasons. Not only can you take a much higher margin on its inference, a margin which may well increase as the tasks performed become more financially critical, but it provides you leverage to make deals for chips, for compute, and for investment that you could not do otherwise. Anyone with compute is financially incentivized to launder it through your model to take a slice of your margins, which gives you massive leverage as its owner.

On June 9th, Anthropic released Fable 5, widely considered to be the most capable model ever released publicly. On the news of the private version of this model, Mythos, Anthropic made a number of quite favorable deals, from leasing xAI’s entire compute capacity at Colossus to raising their Series H at near a trillion dollar valuation. Releasing to the public would allow them to capture the revenue growth to make back their investment into research and training, and to gain even more financial leverage. Going into June, Anthropic's ARR had run from 3 billion to near 50 billion in under a year, on the back of a run of models and harnesses perceived to be at the frontier for enterprise use. After the public release of Opus 4.8, they were adding roughly 400-500M to their ARR every day. When GPT-4 was released, it held the top spot on benchmarks of general capability for nearly a year. For the past year, no model has stayed on top for longer than a couple months. This is the window to entice users and add revenue, and Anthropic knows it. They planned to offer Fable subsidized through subscription plans for two weeks after launch, even given their compute crunch.

On Friday June 12th at 5:21pm, 3 days after release, Anthropic received a letter from the federal government — Fable couldn’t be offered to foreign nationals, effective immediately. Due to the impossibility of complying with this kind of export control immediately, Anthropic was forced to announce its outright removal. By midnight, it was unavailable for all users. On July 1st, 19 days later, access was restored, but the message was sent. With David Sacks as AI czar through late March, the administration had been in favor of a hands-off approach to regulation but this was unsustainable. From Situational Awareness to AI 2027, every popular piece of AI forecasting work has understood that at some point, the government will take more drastic action. When capabilities become dangerous enough, their hand is forced. The first hint of this came from the Department of War’s supply chain risk designation, as they began to consider the military implications of relying on these models. The Fable export control was claimed to be explicitly due to its cyber offensive capabilities. These actions, while somewhat haphazard in implementation, are no anomaly of the current executive. They are evidence of a trend.

Across the US, the involvement of the policy apparatus in the AI industry is exploding.

It is clear with the Fable ban that these measures can directly threaten the core business model of these AI labs. If their stated path to profitability — earning more revenue from each new model than the increased cost to train it — becomes impossible through aggressive restrictions on new model releases, what becomes of their outsized valuations? Compared to the direness of the threat, the labs have spent surprisingly little in effort or money on attempts to influence this policy themselves. If you compare their lobbying spend with other similarly valued companies from different industries, especially the ones with a similar stake in government policy, they are far behind. A company like Lockheed Martin, with a valuation less than 20% that of either main AI lab, spends over 5 times them on lobbying.

In the absence of lobbying, the labs could have also simply endorsed specific policies that they think would be the right way to address the long run problem of dangerous AI capabilities and provided forward guidance on how it will be handled. The first time a lab really took a stab at this was on June 10th, in Dario’s Policy on the AI Exponential. He described how models could be reviewed and controlled, but even this was largely just high level ideas — there was little bite behind the bark. Two days later, Fable was banned. If the labs want to have an influence on the policy around their industry, they will have to fight their battles in the style and on the grounds that the government has set. The same goes for many other outside observers: groups like the AI Futures Project have set the tone for much of the policy talk within San Francisco with projects like AI 2027 and AI 2040, but blog posts can only take you so far. The traditional battles required to affect policy are a different beast, and one few people high in the AI industry can claim experience with.

If labs continue to abdicate on this front, others will continue to fill the void. The amount spent on lobbying for AI issues is skyrocketing. Many of the spenders today are simply firms with experience lobbying and a demonstrated interest in continued acceleration and deregulation from a16z to Meta and Microsoft. OpenAI and Anthropic only spent a combined $6 million on lobbying in 2025, which pales in comparison to the amounts being thrown around in that space now. The recording industry trade association (RIAA) alone spent more lobbying than OpenAI and Anthropic combined, largely looking for copyright protection from AI. If these labs are truly seeking regulation with the stakes they claim and they don’t believe the current policy approaches are the right ones, you would think they would be a bit more involved.

There are a few ways to understand this contradiction. Maybe OpenAI and Anthropic see themselves as aligned with much of the lobbying being done, especially for deregulation, and want to maintain plausible deniability while letting the lobbying towards deregulation continue. This may be true for OpenAI, given Greg Brockman’s personal $25 million contribution to Leading the Future, a notoriously accelerationist political organization. But it would seem surprising for Anthropic, with its history of prioritizing safety and calling for regulation. Perhaps they are genuine, if inexperienced, in their approach to policy — we are seeing them increasingly wade into the messy world of real politics. In just the first half of 2026, they founded the Anthropic Institute, opened their first office in DC, and made a direct $20 million contribution to a political organization (Public First Action), its first major political contribution. I would also wager Anthropic understands that the strategy of scaling up training cost to scale up revenue on each model generation is not one that is sustainable. First, it would risk constant unprofitability while you sink increasing costs into your next model, but as capabilities of the models become dangerous and usage is locked down it might also become difficult to scale up revenue. If they are not able to profit from the direct serving of their models, the labs will look for other ways to make money that may also eventually inspire regulation. They could sell specialized applications like Claude for Design or for Science that surface a harnessed fraction of underlying model capability. More unnervingly, if models become superhuman in certain respects, labs could take advantage of their lead to trade internally or bet on prediction markets. As the core capabilities of the models become more politically salient, political deftness becomes necessary as a core capability of the major AI labs.

For a long time (and maybe still!), one of the most high leverage things you could do in AI was to build a popular benchmark. It took a relatively low capital investment, but required the understanding and taste to find something unique, quantifiable, and generalizable to some fuller sense of a model’s capability level. Labs jostle to see who can score higher on tests like ARC-AGI X or METR’s time horizon benchmark, and in this way the benchmark makers get to help direct AI development without ever being in the room. In a way, policy performs a similar role. As the effects of AI begin to diffuse through our culture and economy, policy can incentivize and restrict the ways this happens — instead of benchmarks defining the axis on which labs compete, these functions will be shaped by law. If the makers of this technology want a say, they will increasingly have to understand how to speak the language, and fight the battles, of traditional politics.

Soon, the public will also demand a say. With the rise of money’s role in politics and the complex opaque dealmaking that has resulted, it has seemed that their leverage has only continued to dwindle. In some crucial ways though, AI may allow people to upend this dynamic and forever change political media, understanding, and activism. The consequences of this transformation will affect not just the labs and AI policy, but political action and engagement across all levels of government, and it’s important that policy makers and the general public are prepared.
