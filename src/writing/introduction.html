After graduating from the University of Michigan, I started my career in software engineering as an individual contributor at Bloomberg 3 years ago. I stayed there for over 2 years, and most of my engineering was on their privileging backend in C++. Like many in my bubble, I had watched my smartest friends go into the AI business out of college, but I didn’t yet have strong opinions on it. I had read some of Yudkowsky and some of the more viral posts on LessWrong, but it was also easy to be dismissive of high level theory on technology that largely didn’t exist.

While at Bloomberg, I quickly formed stronger opinions. There I saw as coding models rapidly improved while being introduced to a lot of the canonical content behind the AI buildout. I became more certain that transformative change was coming, both in my industry and beyond. I wasn’t sure what this would look like. Bloomberg was very cautious about the initial rollout of new models — for good reason, given the downside risk of a failure — which made it hard to reason about the frontier clearly. I started a community for engineers there to come together on AI in coding, which was professionally satisfying but couldn’t make up for the fact that, structurally, I was still bound by a paradigm of software engineering that was actively being disrupted.

I sought out a place where I could fully leverage the frontier models and consider the implications of further progress. I talked to executives at different companies, friends at major AI labs, and some of the students in the maker space I helped run at University of Michigan. Eventually, I landed at a logistics company called Cargomatic. I prioritized being in a domain heavy industry outside of the traditional AI startup ecosystem, while having a lot of leverage to use AI heavily to transform workflows inside the company. I worked out of their San Francisco office, which also let me connect with people I knew who were deeply enmeshed in the AI ecosystem and understand how they were thinking about the world. I can’t talk about exactly what I worked on in detail, but I drove the targets myself. It was quite daunting, especially having to make a lot of decisions in that first week on product direction. I reported directly to the CEO, and I wanted to show quickly what I was capable of. Opus 4.5 launched my second week. By my 5th week, I had written more code there than I had written my entire career at Bloomberg. By my 8th, much of it was in production. I built systems to handle deployment, user onboarding, data storage, security, and built out capabilities to make a variety of tasks more efficient. The deployed cost was, all in, less than $50/month.

Obviously, this was not a completely smooth and continuous process. I handled all communication with the users for what I built out, fixing and improving things as needed. At Bloomberg, I was used to a 2 week turnaround for new code if it wasn’t an urgent fix. Here, there wasn’t much I couldn’t get out the same day if I needed to. Token cost was in no way a hindrance — I was using nearly a billion tokens a month, covered almost entirely by Cursor’s $200/month tier. It was satisfying as someone who loves building things, especially things that I can see make people’s lives easier. However, it was a lot for one person. By my 6th month there, I was pretty burned out. More personally, I was planning a wedding and honeymoon with the woman I have loved for 8 years, and I wanted the time to enjoy that. I also realized that, in a different way than Bloomberg, I was still not able to take the time to reason deeply over the transformative change I had now personally experienced.

I left Cargomatic in May, after spending a little over a month handing my work over. I was married this June. I have spent the time I’ve had thinking and writing. On top of thinking through the rapid change we are in the midst of, I have been considering where I want to invest my time and effort during it to help ensure it goes well. I want to make a lot of that thinking more public, and I will be posting what I can of it here. For those that care, this writing is all done by me, though AI assistance is used for research and review — I do love em-dashes though.

The first series I will be posting will be one on AI policy. At a high level, I think that a good AI future necessitates good AI policy, and that for systemic reasons AI policy has been given less attention than it deserves by the labs, policy makers, and the general public.
