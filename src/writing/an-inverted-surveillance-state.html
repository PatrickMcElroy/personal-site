At a high level, you need three things to make a surveillance state possible: a wealth of accessible data on which to judge an individuals’ actions, a loyal subclass to collect and appraise the data, and a meaningful threat of harm (physical or otherwise) if one is found to be subverting intended behavior. This kind of public scrutiny and monopoly on violence is done to some extent in any society with enforceable laws, making a “surveillance state” more of a difference in degree than kind. The collected data begins to encroach on what is typically seen as more private (conversations with friends, personal mail, the media one chooses to consume), the subclass becomes more hidden than disclosed and is given more authority, and the punishments get more severe. It has been, historically, a massive and cost prohibitive undertaking, but one that is quite effective in influencing peoples’ actions.

There has been a lot of discussion that AI will make the management of a surveillance state significantly easier. The state has rarely lacked in an ability to punish, but rather in the infrastructure of maintaining such wholesale data collection and a privileged subclass to analyze it all and mete out the appropriate punishment. You have to heavily trust in the loyalty of your privileged class, risking internal rule change, while funneling costs from public projects to support your private data collection, risking your external support. Increasingly, the data one needs to collect is largely just digital and so your class can be too. The loyalty and payment of digital minds is much easier to ensure and so, as the logic goes, the bottlenecks to true surveillance authoritarianism will be lost.

While true in isolation, this analysis overlooks the new tools that AI will bring not only to political administrations but to the public as well. In the coming years, unprecedented leverage to surveil and critique their political leaders will be distributed and democratized. It will increase the power of money in our politics, as its contribution can be far more targeted and efficient, while giving voters unparalleled capacity to understand and judge the alignment of politicians with their own priorities.

Assuming democratic politics are maintained, those outside the state are already given the power to threaten through their vote or their dollar. However, the cost of constant analysis on the scale of information generated by the process of policymaking has been out of reach. Federal legislation numbers thousands of pages each year, lobbied by billions of dollars, with thousands of hours of debate and voting throughout. This doesn’t even account for all the constant small and large changes any piece of legislation is subject to before it is passed. The public has therefore always been at the mercy of the media to dissect and interpret this data, who act as their class of privileged analysts. But the people can have no guarantee of loyalty from the media, and the public’s trust for this class has recently fallen to record lows.

At current API prices on both the AI and government API side, to study and interpret the entire output of the Y2025 federal congress (roughly 20,000 pages of the Congressional Record plus the text of some 10,000 introduced bills) would likely cost in the low hundreds of dollars. With this kind of approach, you can control the makeup of your analysis class and the preferences they take into account. You can direct them towards the sources of information you find relevant, deploy parallel agents to ensure a level of consistency, and adjust the level of detail in the outputs of their judgement. Their capabilities will only continue to increase and, more importantly, the cost for a given level of intelligence will fall rapidly. The observed decline in cost for a specified level of model performance has fallen between 9x and 900x per year depending on the task. Even if we take a forward trend at the lower limit of this range, it would imply the ability to run automated analysis on congressional legislation in the cents. Taking a Jevons style view of this, we can imagine that as these costs plummet, the scope of data considered can continue to widen. If it would only cost a few cents to know a politician’s detailed history of written statements and voting action on a certain policy agenda, why not include the entire history of campaign contribution? What if a dollar spent lobbying could be slightly more effective if you knew everything about their family as well? As the costs of outright cyber offense plummet, why not include their private internet traffic or their health records?

As in many places in the AI boom, these effects have been seen in the financial world before the political. At its core, this technique simply allows people to make an opaque environment more legible where there has been a wealth of information but not the proper incentives or ability to analyze it all — or where the ones historically doing the analysis are not trusted to be loyal to your interests. Consider the HayWire, a newsletter that ingests thousands of records of nationwide hay auctions to provide an analysis of how the market pricing is evolving. Historically, the cost to employ a large enough team for this would have been prohibitive for such a niche market. Now that these tools exist, it can be served for free. Another prominent example is simply in trading the financial markets themselves, which has long been characterized by the dominance of the institutional investor over retail. Part of their advantage lies in the tools institutional investor can afford to bring to bear, a margin which may be eroding as the price of increasingly sophisticated AI analysis falls. A paper out of WashU shows a great case study of this; the AI-extracted “sentiment” from earnings calls has been a signal used by institutions to trade against since at least 2010. Looking back before the popularization of AI tools like ChatGPT, we can see that retail’s correlation with this signal was essentially zero. These days, it is about on par with where professional investors were in 2021. Whether this is a good investing signal to use is a separate question, but it is a choice that was not available to the public just a few years ago.

Like all media revolutions before it, this will result in dramatic changes to how politics is done. Prominent politicians will be subject to a level of surveillance leverage unprecedented in the history of democratic societies, and the norms around this new kind of politics are just being born. Are past politically incorrect online posts disqualifying? Will the public see the use of certain personal information as off-limits, like location tracking, analysis of lifelong statement transcripts, or data on family and friends? The change to radio brought us FDR’s fireside chats, television swung in favor of JFK, digital media famously has advantaged Trump. Each step has broadened the access of the public and open the politician up to more scrutiny. What will the process of voting look like with thousands of digital minds at your disposal to scrutinize? What will activism? Or lobbying? Will the government turn off previously public sources of information in response? These are open questions, but ones I largely don’t see the policy world grappling with, as I believe many do not grasp the level of change that is coming

As an attempt to explore this dynamic in the policy field I care about most, I recently launched aipolicypositions.org. The goal of the page is to do what I have proposed in a narrow and rudimentary way. We are not, yet, able to collect and analyze all of the data from our federal government for just cents, and I wanted to avoid having to take any funding from labs or interested groups. I kept the scope down to judging politicians in federal congress and in my home state of Virginia. I also focused on determining only their demonstrated stances for 9 significant AI policy questions.

You can see the methodology page there for more detail, but at a high level this consumes from the full history of relevant public statements by each politician and their voting history on relevant bills. The pipelines for relevance and judgement of support or opposition are fully automated, with parallel reviewers and more intelligent models used in sensitive cases. Personally, I hope to use it to inform my political action, for example by using it to write letters to politicians with certain views or actions. Largely, I also wanted it to be a demonstration of the kind of political action that is rapidly becoming easier to perform.

Even if we do not end up in an AI-enabled authoritarian surveillance state, the potential for a more top down use of these tools will still affect the political landscape. How power is apportioned has increasingly become a function of the effectiveness of advertising and brand. The campaigns that use these tools successfully toward those ends will see massive returns, but the implications of what it could mean in the extremes are quite unnerving. In my next piece I’ll speak on what this world could look like, and how it will change democratic elections.
