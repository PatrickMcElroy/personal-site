One of the first televised political advertisements was Dwight Eisenhower’s famous “Eisenhower Answers America” campaign in the 1952 election. It was the first election where a substantial number of Americans had a television in their homes, and the ad spots played nationwide. With such a general audience, Eisenhower kept the amount of information down and instead (quite successfully) leveraged his own personality and likability. The entire election, he played a total of 40 different TV ads. Twelve years later, in 1964, LBJ still only sponsored 25. From 1952 to today, we have seen a combination of an explosion in campaign finance paired with plummeting costs of media creation. It has made possible a world where the average piece of political advertising for a presidential election is rigorously tested and targeted, even compared to just a decade ago. If we look at the sum of distinct advertisements, we can see the intense acceleration in ad count hastened along by each new medium.

AI offers the campaign the ability to quickly make this count even higher. The cost of media creation is quickly nearing zero, making the cost to advertise collapse to the limit of the cost of the required data and ad hosting medium — both of which are falling as well. Even just limited to media, this will allow an unprecedented level of ad individualization as automated customized ads based on detailed user data become feasible. This is just the baseline and, in some ways, it is already happening. What we may see soon are models able to infiltrate the digital space of specific groups or people to persuade and convince, varying their approach infinitely on the exact frontier of efficient political influence.

In the US especially, a huge proportion of political capital is spent in just a few areas. The structure of our presidential electoral college and district based seating makes few counties at all competitive. Campaigns quickly realized that the traditional approach of standardized advertising could be subverted by filming spots specifically for these districts highlighting policies that are known to play well there and attacking opponents for transgressions that don’t. Much of the variation we’ve already seen has been motivated by this dynamic, and this won’t change while the political math doesn’t. Politically, an individual in a swing state district matters more than those in a safe one by an order of magnitude. While these individuals keep their political premium, there will keep being a premium on the amount campaigns are willing to spend to change their mind. AI paired with sophisticated data collection performs two crucial tasks for the motivated campaign. It allows them to select their targets more effectively — an undecided voter in a swing district is worth infinitely more than one where no amount of money could change their vote. It also lets them vary their ads even more selectively, by automatically creating media for undecided voters based on their individual intuited interests and priorities. If we know Bob loves to play video games, why not serve an ad showing how your political opponent could increase pricing in gaming hardware? Why not include some AI generated footage of them with people you know Bob has a negative opinion of?

This idea is not one that has been wholly unexplored. Many existing political ads contain AI generated footage or are algorithmically served. Hackenburg et al. performed a study on the stronger variant — where LLMs create micro targeted advertising themselves — and found that language models could not outperform even generic political messages in political persuasion. However, there are some key flaws in the methodology. Firstly, it only considered the performance of a baseline GPT-4, with no persuasion specific fine tuning, and it did not compare results across model generations. The level of user knowledge the model had was also limited to simple answers on 10 basic questions (age, gender, party, etc.) — not approaching the wealth of detail on users available from data collectors. The team did eventually investigate the issue of model generation and found in a later piece that performance was correlated with generation and that a bit of post training could make the model remarkably more effective. The study also found something else significant — when the model was given a conversational mode to persuade the user, its performance beat out simple messaging by a wide margin.

The idea of superhuman persuasion is not a new one in the AI space. It has been forecast as one of the most telling capabilities of future superintelligence, along with superhuman forecasting. The timeline of arrival of these abilities is a key dividing line between two of the major camps in AI — the “AI as a Normal Technology” folks and those with views similar to “AI 2027”. We are approaching the line where this distinction will become very clear. If AIs will be able to persuade at superhuman levels soon, the value of digital conversational interface with undecided voters will skyrocket. Even if they do merely level off close to human level, the ability to perform political persuasion at scale and with access to detailed user data would in itself be a revolution in campaign effectiveness.

AI seems unlikely to level off at human performance, and we already have evidence that it hasn’t.

Some of the same authors released a paper last month simply titled “AI systems out-persuade expert humans,” showing in their studied context that frontier models could beat even professional human debaters. We do not currently know what the limit of this Y axis is — how good at persuading could AI possibly get? Certainly they could get good enough to know what kind of digital media or image might be effective to convince a given voter, well enough to perform the kind of micro targeted media we discussed. How reliable could they get in persuading an undecided voter to vote for a specified candidate with 5 minutes of 1:1 conversation? What about even a voter on the other side of the aisle? We don’t know, but the campaigns that find any measure of success will quickly enjoy a massive advantage against those that don’t.

Even if our politics does not devolve fully into competing AI super persuaders, it seems clear that the technology will have some place in the future of political campaigning. We will see this frontier pushed, as always, in the battleground states. Increasingly, the frontier will also be the battleground people — the subset with characteristics associated with a voter who would be worth the time and money to attempt to persuade. Policymakers should think about the kind of campaigns they want to run and consider the tradeoffs of entering into this kind of regime. The same studies found that models will often trade off accuracy when optimized for persuasion, and if we allow campaigns to be run by these kinds of systems it raises the question of the worth of public politics in itself. We may soon find ourselves stuck in a collective action trap similar to the bind many politicians find themselves in with funding following Citizens United — refuse the tools of modern campaigning and lose, or sacrifice your integrity and honesty for the chance to win.

We are entering into a world that may soon look unrecognizable, but the rules and foundations of that world get to be laid today. It may be our last chance to get them right.
