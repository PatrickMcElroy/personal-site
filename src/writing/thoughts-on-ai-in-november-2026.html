I recently went to an AI safety workshop (GCP London, which I would highly recommend!) and had the chance to talk with a lot of amazing people about the current trajectory of AI. I wanted to write out a lot of messy thoughts I have from the weekend while it's still fresh, especially because we got two other rather large pieces of AI news while I was there – the METR report and the Jensen spy scandal.1

The following collection of opinions are all weakly held, and I would appreciate good faith critique. I say this in a personal capacity and these views do not reflect the views of any organization.

Most current technical 'safety' research has the outcome of increasing the frontier of economically productive capabilities and increasing long-horizon x-risk.

Similar to AI Control May Increase Existential Risk, a lot of prosaic alignment research or control schemes do not reduce long-run existential risk. Rather, they allow the meta-entity of the AI industry to continue profitably increasing capabilities without risking immediate harm. Each continuous step forward in controlled, productively diffused AI capabilities without a step-change in our ability to align models increases the stakes of any single failure. Let's reduce risk through regulation instead – to which the current bottleneck is political will, not research.

We will soon see evidence of a substantial "pulling away" of the US from China

The effects that have caused a perceived "narrowing" between the US and China – namely, distillation, chip smuggling, and US frontier model regulation – seem unlikely to remain as significant. OpenAI and Anthropic are keeping their most capable models private for longer, reducing the ability for them to be distilled.2 Even for released models, both companies have started viciously fighting distillation campaigns in a way that seems to be working. Many of China's best labs have become overly reliant on methods of training that may be obsolete within the month. The attack on Railway for older API keys to generate model traces for distillation may be the last gasp of this regime. This comes as Anthropic's internal models cross their RSP threshold for automated AI R&D3 and Jensen's affair cuts down on the ability for chips to be smuggled into China. The combination of these effects, along with the dramatic acceleration Anthropic seems to be experiencing (METR report), indicates we may see a radical expansion of the US lead. We are seeing a bit of an expansion already.

The selection effects for powerful organizations, even in safety specific organizations, struggle to select those with short timelines even if these views are high-status

I see a valid concern of a kind of groupthink in AI safety spaces where people in the space see a belief in shorter timelines as more 'high status,' thus pushing people towards this view regardless of its epistemic strength. However, I worry that another dynamic exists pushing the other way – namely, that optimism and high-agency tend to be correlated. High-agency is both strongly selected for in AI safety spaces and itself correlated with professional competence. In this way, people with more 'optimistic' views on a variety of axes (the feasibility of alignment, the length of timelines, the likelihood of a bilateral international agreement) tend to be selected for and given more influence, regardless of whether they are right. To me, this is somewhat the story of the AI industry writ large – the AI CEOs are all optimistic and high-agency, simply on different axes. This would be less worrying if we weren't playing in epistemic sudden death.

I am uncertain on my own timelines. I really hope that AI Futures Project is wrong, but unfortunately their analysis seems credible.

If their update from yesterday is correct, we are likely to see an intelligence explosion in 2027. This is quite a shift from their timelines as of just 6 months ago, a period over which I have not personally updated as much on broad capability trends. I considered Eli's 2035 TED-AI estimate from earlier this year longer than my own. Now that he's at 2028, I'm very uncertain and worried. As a group, they have a history of accurate forecasting in this domain that is hard to argue against.

US AI legislation will be needed before the next presidential election, which means it needs to be considered carefully now

Waiting until after 2028 to pass comprehensive AI regulation is unacceptable given the kind of timelines that seem possible. If it is to be passed in the next legislative session, it needs a few qualities:

  • Fits the perceived 'mandate' of the Democratic Congress, which Democrats view as fighting corruption and maintaining affordability. The Jensen Huang/Trump corruption scandal alongside voter datacenter cost concerns may provide the perfect backdrop for some kind of AI legislation.
  • Does more than 'signal to voters' before being vetoed or blocked. It will need Trump's direct support or to be baked into a must-pass vehicle. One must remember that Trump cares about his legacy above all, less so the Republican Party – selling him as the potential savior of the human race might genuinely work.
  • Operationalizes the actual verbiage. It can't just be a vague "ban" on "RSI" or "superintelligence" if we want it to be truly binding.

In terms of operationalizing verbiage, a decent stand-in for an "RSI ban" could be a requirement to verify that internal R&D inference is not using unreleased models.

In order to keep business secrets private, this could use something like the new FlashProve ZKP approach Anthropic released. I'm hoping to write more on this soon!

Assumptions of short timelines in the AI safety space justify actions that may be detrimental in long timelines worlds

Many of the largest grant-makers and organizations in AI safety agree that the writing is on the wall for superintelligence arriving in the next few years. This means many will switch from building up capital to cashing it in, across a variety of axes: burning political capital to say things outside the Overton window, burning grant money to fund moonshots now, burning social capital by potentially alienating a public which does not hold these views. To be clear, this very much seems (to me) to be justified based on rational forecasting! But if the chips are pushed to the middle and you lose, buying back in might be very expensive.

The UK is punching far above its weight class in AI safety

This has been obvious for a while, but even with this in mind before visiting, I was surprised by the depth of talent there. Without a single frontier lab4, the UK has the best government AI division (UK AISI), one of the best third party auditors (Apollo Research), and a thriving ecosystem of new research and policy startups. It's a shame they won't have more hard power to back them!

We will see cyberattacks start to cause more immediate effects on people's lives this winter

The UK and Japan just officially recommended people stock up on food and water. Officially, they cited "ordinary disruptions" and "natural disasters," but it is notable that the UK and Japan are the two countries most briefed on the potential for imminent catastrophic cyberattacks (through UK AISI and the recent attacks on Japanese cyber infrastructure respectively). We have already seen some hacks of supermarkets and blood banks that have been relatively easy to ignore. I think governments have good reason to expect we will soon see an incident that will not be as easy to ignore.

I need to be careful where I compartmentalize my thoughts on AI risk

It is easy, sometimes necessary, to separate the part of my brain living normal life from the part that thinks we could see catastrophic harms from superintelligent AI within the next year. Part of the reason I moved back to my hometown was to enjoy normal time with my family. It's easy to want this time to be 'normal,' and to not let my crazy ideas about the future mar the enjoyment of life in the moment now. But there is a limit to this – I would certainly feel stupid to be predicting a cyberattack on civilian infrastructure only to experience one without any water or food stocked up.

"Humans in the cloud" is a key way AI safety people talk past the rest of the world5

I find this term a bit annoying since it is a bit of a contradiction in terms.6 Part of what it means to be human is a bodily existence – even an outright brain upload would not quite be human anymore. The AIs we create, regardless of their top-line intelligence or data efficiency, will not be able to draw on the same senses we do, the same sense of our human mortality and memory. And people do really care about this shared human experience! Even if people like an AI piece of art better than a human one, they will change their minds when the authors are revealed. Where humans maintain control over various economic decisions – what products to buy, what companies to invest in, what technology to use – we should expect this to drive massive value to humans in a Baumol-like way. So is AI a normal technology then? I still think no, for two big reasons. First, much of AI research (and scientific research in general) has no human bottleneck, so a software singularity may come before economic diffusion. Second, this dynamic only remains while humans are in control. Disempowerment of these human economic decisions to AI will be massively incentivized. Once AIs are being used to make better purchasing decisions faster, the other side of the transaction is pushed to use the same tools to sell to it. Even if we don't have "humans in the cloud," we could still have RSI, superintelligence, and radical economic disempowerment. There may be plenty of artisanal work left, but all human work will be artisanal.

On the whole, the people I met interested in and working on AI safety were genuine, kind, and intelligent.

Thanks to everyone I met at GCP London and to the organizers for doing such a great job bringing in amazing guests and participants. From those I talked to already working in the space, the picture I have of AI safety is one with a lot of genuinely worried people doing their best. With the stakes being as they perceive them, they are working themselves quite hard. The other participants there alongside me all brought a lot of great perspective from longstanding policy or professional experience. I was really impressed on the whole by the epistemic humility everyone brought to the table given their level of expertise and the weirdness of the conversations. It was exciting for me to see this, given that the (relatively low) tractability of AI safety to existing powerful institutions is something I really worry about. If people with experience working with those institutions can be so open and intellectually curious, it makes me more optimistic about the ability to effect change within them.
